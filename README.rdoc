== Ship a Ruby App to People Who Don't Have Ruby

You wrote a Ruby program for people who have never installed Ruby and
never will. Fledge[https://github.com/setdef/Fledge] turns it into an app
they download and open like any other: a Mac app on a disk image, a Windows
setup program, a Linux AppImage, or a command they install with Homebrew or
winget. For iPhone, iPad, and Android, Fledge writes the Xcode or Gradle
project you build the app from.

You write your app in Ruby, with whatever front end your framework
uses, on every platform, phones included, and never the native code a
platform needs: Fledge supplies every line of it.

Your code runs as it does when you type <tt>ruby main.rb</tt> or
<tt>bundle exec</tt>, with the same requires, globals, and standard
library, and the gem versions in your +Gemfile.lock+, compiled gems
included. If your users happen to have Ruby, your app ignores it, and its
settings. Your code can see a few
{differences}[https://github.com/setdef/Fledge/blob/trunk/doc/differences_from_ruby.md],
each listed with its reason.

Fledge runs on Ruby 4.1, with Bundler, and until Ruby 4.1.0 is released,
on a preview of it, which the Ruby badge on the README shows. Add it to
your project's +Gemfile+:

  gem "fledge", git: "https://github.com/setdef/Fledge", tag: "v0.17.0", group: :development

Each release also carries the <tt>.gem</tt> built from its tag, attached to
it as <tt>fledge-X.Y.Z.gem</tt>, which <tt>gem install</tt> takes from a
file if a registry or git host is down.

=== Your First App

In a folder named +greet+, a two-line +main.rb+ prints a greeting as JSON,
and a +Gemfile+ holds the line above. Fledge builds the app for the
computer you run it on. On a Mac with Apple silicon, the build, and a run
of the app, look like this:

  $ bundle exec fledge build
  dist/Greet-arm64-darwin.tar.gz
  $ tar -xzf dist/Greet-arm64-darwin.tar.gz -C /tmp
  $ /tmp/Greet-arm64-darwin/Greet Ruby
  {"greeting":"Hello, Ruby!"}

The app carries a whole Ruby runtime, so the archive and the unpacked
folder are tens of megabytes: <tt>du -sh dist/Greet-arm64-darwin</tt> shows
the size of the folder you built. Anyone with an Apple silicon Mac on
macOS 26 or newer can run it, whether or not they have Ruby. On Windows, the same command
writes <tt>dist/Greet-x64-mingw-ucrt.zip</tt>, which holds +Greet.exe+, and
on Linux, a <tt>.tar.gz</tt> for your processor, such as
<tt>dist/Greet-x86_64-linux.tar.gz</tt>.

That app holds Fledge too, as it holds your other development gems,
because Bundler installs a gem from git as its whole repository. To see its
size, build the app both ways and compare <tt>du -sh</tt> of the two folders
in +dist+. You can't leave Fledge out with Bundler's +without+ setting,
because <tt>without development</tt> would leave it out of your bundle as
well. To build apps without it, put the line above in a +Gemfile+ in a
folder outside your project, run <tt>bundle install</tt> there, and from
that folder, build your project by its path:
<tt>bundle exec fledge build ~/code/greet</tt>. An app built this way
holds no copy of Fledge.

=== What You Get

Fledge ships the gems <tt>bundle exec</tt> would load, from every group in
your +Gemfile+, +development+ and +test+ included. To leave a group out,
name it in Bundler's +without+ setting. A gem's precompiled build ships as
it is when one fits your platform and admits the app's Ruby. When none
does, Fledge compiles the gem on your computer: a gem published only as
source, a gem from git, and a gem whose precompiled builds all require
another Ruby.

[Terminal apps]
  A <tt>.tar.gz</tt> on macOS and Linux, and a <tt>.zip</tt> on Windows,
  each with its SHA-256 beside it, for Scoop, Homebrew, winget,
  Chocolatey, and the AUR.
[Windowed apps]
  A <tt>.app</tt> and a <tt>.dmg</tt> on macOS; a <tt>.zip</tt>, a setup
  program, and, if you ask, an MSIX package for the Microsoft Store on
  Windows; and a <tt>.tar.gz</tt> with a desktop entry, an AppImage, and,
  if you ask, a <tt>.deb</tt> on Linux. Each system shows your app's name,
  icon, and version, and offers your app for the files you name.
[Phones]
  An Xcode or Gradle project for iPhone, iPad, and Android, whose screen
  your Ruby can draw with each phone's widgets, UIKit's and Android's
  views, with your gems' C, C++, and Rust extensions compiled for the
  phone, and the +ffi+ gem and Ruby's +fiddle+ working there.
[Native widgets]
  Your Ruby can draw each system's widgets, as it would under
  <tt>ruby main.rb</tt>, with +fiddle+, which Ruby bundles, on the main
  thread: AppKit on a Mac, GTK 4 and libadwaita on Linux, WinUI 3 or
  Win32's controls on Windows, UIKit on iPhone and iPad, and Android's
  views on Android. Fledge adds no widget, library, or binding for it.
  The {Basket}[https://github.com/setdef/Fledge/tree/trunk/examples/basket#readme]
  example is a shopping list that does so on all five. An app that draws
  with GTK 4 needs GTK 4 and libadwaita on the Linux computer, and one
  that draws with WinUI 3 needs the Windows App Runtime on the Windows
  one; the app carries neither.
[Signing]
  <tt>--sign</tt> signs every program, library, bundle, and installer
  with your macOS identity or your Windows certificate, and
  <tt>--notarize</tt> has Apple notarize a Mac app.
[Frameworks]
  <tt>fledge build --from</tt> makes a new app from one Fledge already
  built, with no Bundler, network, or compiler on the computer.

Fledge builds for macOS 26 and newer on Apple silicon; Linux with glibc
2.28 and newer, or with musl, on x86-64 and ARM64; Windows 11 on x64 and
ARM64; iOS 15 and newer; and Android 8.0 and newer. It builds nothing for
Intel Macs, 32-bit systems, or the Mac App Store. Build each system's app
on a computer of that system, such as a CI runner of each. On Linux, a gem
compiled on your computer may need your computer's newer glibc, and then
so does your app: <tt>fledge build</tt> warns you, and building on an
older Linux, such as AlmaLinux 8, lowers it.

=== How Fledge Compares

To run a program you distribute as a gem, people have to install a
compatible version of Ruby, and then install your gem. With
{Traveling Ruby}[https://github.com/trubygems/traveling-ruby]'s +truby+,
you can package your app for every system from one computer, but you can
use a gem with a C extension only if Traveling Ruby built that extension
for its release, and your users get an archive and a script to start.

{Ocran}[https://github.com/largo/ocran] packs your app into one
executable, and you have to change your code for it: your program runs
from a temporary folder, and Ocran runs it while it builds. Every time
someone opens an Ocran app, they wait while it unpacks.
{Tebako}[https://github.com/tamatebako/tebako] packs your app into one
executable too. The first time someone opens a Tebako app, it downloads
its Ruby into a folder that every Tebako app on that computer shares, and
it runs on the newest Ruby there, which may be one you never tried.
Neither makes anything for phones.

With Fledge, you change nothing in your code, and you keep the gems it
uses today, because Fledge compiles them on each system you ship to. Your
app carries the Ruby you built it with, and your users open it with no
internet connection and no wait while anything unpacks.

Fledge is 0.x, so any new version can break your build: read the changelog
before you update.
